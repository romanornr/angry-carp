# Email phishing detection: research review

Reviewed: 2026-09-21. Scope: representative primary research, not an exhaustive survey or a detector selection. No private mail, candidate websites, or remote scanner submissions were used. Dates below are publication or first-submission dates, not search-engine crawl dates.

## Recommendation

Evaluate a layered design: deterministic local evidence extraction, an optional inexpensive classifier for prioritization, and model-assisted assessment that cites the actual evidence. Keep reporting eligibility and approval separate from detection scores. This is a design recommendation from the evidence below, not a claim that this combination has already been validated for Angry Carp.

Do not train a custom model first. Establish a labeled evaluation set and compare a simple baseline, one configured LLM, and their combination. Training, local inference, and hosted inference are separate privacy decisions: local inference can avoid sending message contents to a provider; explanation generation or fine-tuning through an API can disclose the same contents even when the final detector runs locally.

## Six useful papers

### 1. Learning to Detect Phishing Emails — WWW, May 2007

PILFER combines hand-designed email features with a random forest. Its observations include displayed-link/destination mismatches, URL structure, domain age, and spam-filter output. Evaluation used ten-fold cross-validation over approximately 860 phishing messages and 6,950 legitimate messages from separate source corpora. The authors disabled historical blacklist lookups to avoid using knowledge unavailable when messages arrived. [Paper, sections 3–4](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~tomasic/doc/2007/FetteSadehTomasicWWW2007.pdf)

**Use:** a historical example of inspectable feature extraction and time-appropriate evidence. **Limit:** old data, class correlated with corpus source, and cross-validation that does not establish performance on later campaigns. Reuse the idea of explicit observations; do not import historical scores or assume every feature is suspicious by itself.

**Dot-count clarification (2026-09-23):** Section 3.2.8 uses the maximum number of literal `.` characters in any link in the email, including dots outside the hostname. The examples cover misleading subdomains and URLs embedded in redirect parameters. This is one numeric feature in the classifier, not a standalone phishing threshold. The authors explicitly acknowledge that legitimate URLs also contain multiple dots. Angry Carp does not implement this feature or the PILFER classifier. Its domain comparisons instead retain full hostnames, use public-suffix parsing and compare names against supplied references.

### 2. Detecting and Characterizing Lateral Phishing at Scale — USENIX Security, August 2019

Studies phishing sent from compromised enterprise accounts. Its random forest uses recipient behavior, message content, and URL reputation. Training covers April–June 2018; testing covers July–October with additional held-out organizations and monthly updates using earlier observations. This is a more relevant evaluation pattern than random message splitting. [Author-hosted paper, sections 3–5](https://www.icir.org/vern/papers/lat-phish-usec19.pdf)

**Use:** contextual signals and testing on genuinely later traffic. **Limit:** enterprise-wide telemetry and URL-based lateral phishing are different from one personal inbox. Ground truth combines user reports and detector discoveries, so undiscovered attacks can remain unknown. A familiar sender is insufficient proof of safety; an unfamiliar one is insufficient proof of phishing. Broad enterprise accuracy cannot be transferred to Angry Carp.

### 3. E-PhishGen: Unlocking Novel Research in Phishing Email Detection — first submitted September 2025; ACM AISec 2025

Compares TF-IDF classifiers, DistilBERT, and prompted LLMs across existing email corpora, then introduces a synthetic multilingual benchmark. Five repeated stratified 70:30 splits and cross-corpus experiments show that strong same-corpus performance can deteriorate on different data. The authors identify old English-heavy datasets and spam mislabeled as phishing. [Paper, sections 2–5](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.01791v2), [venue metadata](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.01791), [authors' code](https://github.com/pajola/e-phishGen)

**Use:** the most directly relevant starting point for comparing simple ML, smaller transformers, and LLMs. **Limit:** random splits do not establish campaign-separated generalization; synthetic email quality and generator style limit realism. A new dataset publication date does not establish that its underlying messages are recent. Its synthetic corpus is a stress-test candidate, not sufficient evidence of real inbox performance.

### 4. Improving Phishing Email Detection Performance of Small Large Language Models — April 2025 preprint

Uses LoRA and explanations generated by GPT-4o-mini to fine-tune small models, comparing them with embedding-based traditional classifiers. Evaluation uses a held-out SpamAssassin test set of 1,069 messages. [Paper, sections 3–4](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.00034v1)

**Use:** a possible later experiment for local inference. **Limit:** SpamAssassin is a spam/ham source; this paper does not establish the task's equivalence to independently labeled phishing. Table 1 also reports precision/recall pairs inconsistent with the stated F1 formula: for example, 0.934 and 0.922 imply approximately 0.928, not the reported 0.543. Treat headline scores as unresolved, not a reason to select this method. Its training pipeline sends emails to a hosted explanation model; local final inference does not remove that earlier disclosure.

### 5. LLM-PEA: Leveraging Large Language Models Against Phishing Email Attacks — December 2025 preprint

Tests model/prompt choices, paraphrasing, instruction injection, and translation on email text. Includes 2,000 balanced examples, a 200-message imbalanced set, and synthetic attack variants. Its methods exclude responses that cannot be parsed into a classification. [Paper, sections III–IV](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.10104v1)

**Use:** adversarial text and multilingual evaluation belong in the test plan. **Limit:** selected text datasets and synthesized variants do not establish operational performance. Excluding malformed outputs hides an important deployment failure mode. Some metric increments also appear inconsistent with the stated number of phishing examples; avoid ranking providers from these results. Angry Carp should count unusable answers and abstentions explicitly, and evaluate Dutch as well as English independently.

### 6. LLMail-Inject: A Dataset from a Realistic Adaptive Prompt Injection Challenge — June 2025 preprint

Evaluates an email assistant whose retrieved messages can attempt to trigger unauthorized sending or disclosure. This is an agent-security benchmark, not a phishing classifier benchmark. It tests adaptive attacks against multiple defenses and distinguishes generated tool calls from successful end-to-end attacks. [Paper, sections 2, 4, and 6](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.09956v1), [authors' analysis repository](https://github.com/microsoft/llmail-inject-challenge-analysis)

**Use:** test whether hostile email can cause an action, not just whether a classifier recognizes it. **Limit:** fixed attack objectives and partly model-annotated subsets limit generalization; the authors explicitly discourage directly training on this dataset. For Angry Carp, message content must not authorize scanning, destination selection, disclosure, or sending. Prompt instructions and an injection detector are additional defenses, not substitutes for tool-level policy and operator approval.

## Evaluation proposal for Angry Carp

The following is proposed work; none of these experiments has been run here.

1. Label phishing, ordinary spam, legitimate mail, and unresolved cases separately. Include ordinary unfamiliar mail, legitimate signing requests, forwarding, and compromised trusted senders. Keep supporting evidence and adjudication history with each label.
2. Keep exact duplicates and near-identical campaign templates together. Train/tune on earlier material; evaluate on later messages and campaigns absent from training. Fit preprocessing on training data only. Freeze dataset versions, labels, model versions, prompts, and the time at which intelligence became available.
3. Compare deterministic observations alone, a simple local text/feature classifier, one LLM, and a selective combination. Include an ablation that removes each signal family so complexity must demonstrate value. Evaluation of a fixed workflow versus a free-running agent is a separate comparison.
4. Measure phishing precision and recall, false alerts per thousand legitimate messages, review load, abstentions, malformed outputs, latency, and cost. Report by language, campaign, message type, and acquisition source; report uncertainty rather than only a headline accuracy.
5. Test with a realistic phishing prevalence. Illustrative arithmetic: among 10,000 messages with 1% phishing, 90% recall and 1% false-positive rate produce 90 true alerts and 99 false alerts—only about 48% precision. These are hypothetical values, not a paper result.
6. Test prompt injection, Unicode substitutions, obfuscated text, and text hidden in HTML without rendering remote content. Preserve original bytes; altered views and normalized features are separately identified derivatives. Treat image/attachment-only content as a coverage gap until a safe extraction capability is designed.
7. Separately evaluate report quality: are factual claims supported, is the destination verified, has private data been removed, and is the exact report approved? A classification benchmark cannot validate provider routing, successful remediation, actor attribution, or campaign size.

## Privacy and evidence boundaries

Public benchmark availability does not authorize uploading private originals or reusing them for training. Keep evaluation data local unless a specific disclosure has been approved. Hosted-model assessment still sends the supplied excerpts or features to that provider; this needs a distinct policy from private URL scanning. Redaction must preserve enough context for assessment and explicitly record what was withheld.

These papers do not justify direct fetching of candidate sites, scanning every link, or automatically accusing a provider based on a model score. Email evidence can support claims about the email; it does not establish unseen website behavior. Report preparation still requires the project's evidence standard, and every initial-version send still requires operator approval.
